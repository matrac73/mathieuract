On 5 November, the Bank of England announced that it was raising its policy rate from 3.75% to 4%. It is its first increase in more than three years. Eight of the nine committee members voted for this quarter point, and the ninth, Catherine Mann, wanted to go straight to 4.25%.

## What happened

The policy rate is the rate at which the central bank lends to banks. When it goes up, credit becomes more expensive for everyone, households and firms spend less, and prices rise more slowly.

The reason for the increase is inflation, meaning the rise in prices over twelve months. It was 2.6% in June and 3.5% in September, against a target of 2%. The Bank projects about 4.5% in the first quarter of 2027 and about 5% in the second. The cause is known: the conflict in the Middle East is entering its ninth month, a barrel of Brent trades at around 108 dollars, and UK gas futures cost 81% more than in July. Motor fuel and energy bills alone account for about 1 point of September's 3.5%.

There is something odd here. An interest rate in London changes nothing about the price of oil. To understand what the Bank is trying to do, I go back to a notion from my second-year economics course.

## The Phillips curve and what shifts it

The course presents the Phillips curve as a decreasing curve between inflation and unemployment. When few people are looking for a job, firms have to pay more to hire, and prices follow. When unemployment is high, the opposite happens. During the post-war boom, this curve served as an instruction manual, the "stop and go" policy: the economy was stimulated when unemployment rose and slowed when inflation rose. Governments picked a point on the curve.

The 1970s broke this manual. After the oil shocks came stagflation, which the course defines as rising unemployment in an inflationary environment. Both problems at once, which the curve ruled out.

The explanation kept by the course is Friedman's: workers who anticipate inflation ask for a pay rise so as not to lose purchasing power. So the curve is not fixed. It depends on the inflation everyone expects, and it moves up as a whole when that expectation rises.

Here is a numerical example, which I make up for illustration and which does not describe the UK economy. I take the curve: inflation = expected inflation + 20/unemployment − 4, with unemployment in percent. If people expect 2% inflation, unemployment at 5% does give 2%. If they expect 4%, the same unemployment gives 4%. To get back to 2% without changing expectations, we need 20/unemployment = 2, so unemployment at 10%.

```figure
file: fig-2.en.svg
caption: An illustrative Phillips curve, before and after a rise in inflation expectations
scroll: A first curve is drawn, a point slides along it, then a second, higher curve appears and the point has to travel far to the right to reach the target again.
steps:
- The Phillips curve links unemployment and inflation. Here, when everyone expects prices to rise by 2%, unemployment at 5% gives 2% inflation, which is point A.
- "Stop and go" means moving along this curve. At A′, one point less unemployment is paid for with one point more inflation.
- If everyone expects a 4% rise and asks for the matching wage, the whole curve moves up. With the same unemployment, inflation goes from A to B.
- On this new curve, getting back to 2% inflation means going all the way to C, which is doubled unemployment in this example.
```

## What the Bank is trying to prevent

Read this way, the decision becomes consistent. The first rise in prices, that of petrol and gas, is already here and the rate can do nothing about it. What the Bank is watching is what comes next, which it calls second-round effects: workers asking for rises because they expect 5% inflation, firms passing those wages on into their prices, and an inflation that carries on even if oil comes back down. That is the shift of the curve.

The minutes say there is still limited evidence of these effects, but that the risk has risen. Wage growth and services price inflation, which had been slowing, have stopped slowing in recent months. Several members point to the 2027 pay round as the key test. One of them, Alan Taylor, speaks of an insurance hike: paying a little now so as not to have to go and find point C later.

```figure
file: fig-3.en.svg
caption: The price-wage loop, and where the policy rate acts
scroll: The chain starts from energy, goes down to wages, closes into a loop, then the policy rate comes in to slow that loop without touching energy.
steps:
- First round. Energy costs more and consumer prices rise. This is mechanical and already under way.
- If households expect this to last, they ask for pay rises to make up for it.
- Firms pass these wages on into their prices. The loop is closed and inflation feeds itself, which is the second round.
- The rate at 4% does not change the price of energy. It makes credit more expensive and demand weaker, which slows the loop.
```

The course adds a point that sheds light on the tone of the statement. According to it, monetary orthodoxy consists in setting a credible rule and submitting to it. If everyone believes the central bank will bring inflation back to 2%, nobody has a reason to ask for 5%, and the curve does not move. A small rate increase therefore also works as a signal.

## What the notion does not say

The Phillips curve does not say how far to raise. The course cites the Taylor rule, which makes the rate depend on the gap between inflation and its target and on the gap between growth and its potential. Here the two gaps do not pull with the same strength: inflation is well above target, but most members judge that the economy still has spare capacity. Hence the disagreement between a quarter and a half point.

Nor does it say what the increase will cost. The course recalls that stabilising inflation in the 1980s weighed heavily on growth in France. The Bank itself states that its policy is not on a pre-set path. The next decision is due on 17 December, and what I take away is that the question asked that day will be less about oil than about what people in the UK think of next year's prices.
