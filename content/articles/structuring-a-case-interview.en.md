A case interview has no easy answer, and that is by design. The interviewer is not checking whether you find the number; they are watching how you bring order to a messy question. Structure is the whole exercise.

Here is the method I settled on after working through a stack of practice cases.

## Step 1: clarify before you structure

The brief is always incomplete on purpose. Before drawing anything, I ask a few questions:

- **The objective.** Growth, profit, return on investment? By how much, and by when? "Increase revenue by 50% in five years" is a different case from "grow".
- **The business.** What does the company sell, to whom, through which channels?
- **The constraints.** Budget, time horizon, appetite for risk, things the client refuses to do.

Then I restate the question in one sentence and check that the interviewer agrees. It takes thirty seconds and prevents solving the wrong problem.

## Step 2: break the question into sub-questions

The core move is to split the main question into sub-questions that are:

- **Focused on the problem**: every branch helps answer *this* question, not a generic one.
- **Neutral**: they open an analysis rather than assume a conclusion. No recommendation before the facts.
- **MECE**: mutually exclusive (no overlap) and collectively exhaustive (no gap).

There are two ways to build the tree:

- **Top-down**, from a formula or a known logic. Profit = revenue − costs; revenue = price x volume; costs = fixed + variable. Mathematical trees are MECE by construction.
- **Bottom-up**, by listing ideas first and then grouping them into coherent buckets. Useful for open, qualitative questions.

I ask for a minute of silence to draw the tree, then present it top line first: "I would look at three areas...", before going into any of them.

## The recurring case types

Most cases are variations on a small number of questions. Knowing the natural skeleton of each saves time:

| Question | Natural starting structure |
|---|---|
| Profit is falling | Revenue (price, volume, mix) versus costs (fixed, variable) |
| How to grow | Existing versus new products, existing versus new customers or markets |
| Should we enter this market | Market attractiveness, competition, our capabilities, economics |
| Should we invest or acquire | Stand-alone value, synergies, price, risks |
| How much is this worth | Expected revenues, costs, timing, discounting |
| What price to set | Cost-based, competitor-based, value-based |

A warning: these are starting points, not templates to recite. A framework applied without adapting it to the case is the most visible mistake a candidate can make.
## Step 3: prioritise and state a hypothesis

A full tree cannot be explored in thirty minutes. After presenting it, I say where I would start and why: "Costs have risen faster than prices according to the brief, so I would start there."

Leading with a hypothesis turns the case from a questionnaire into an investigation. If the data contradict it, drop it out loud and move to the next branch. Changing your mind in front of evidence is a point in your favour.

## Step 4: analyse, and say "so what"

Each calculation or chart should end with its meaning for the client. Not "the segment is worth 3 billion", but "the segment is worth 3 billion, so capturing 3% of it would close our growth gap, which looks achievable".

For creative questions, structure still applies: group ideas into two or three families rather than listing ten in a row.

## Step 5: close like you are in a lift

The final synthesis is short and answer-first:

1. **The question**, in one sentence.
2. **The recommendation**: a clear position, with a number where possible.
3. **The two or three reasons** that support it.
4. **Risks and next steps.**

A recommendation can be "no". In one investment case the return on paper beat the alternative, but the absolute gain was small, the forecast depended on one fragile assumption, and it required the owner's time. Saying "not yet, and here is what I would check first" is a legitimate answer.

## Mistakes I made, so you do not have to

- Starting to structure before understanding the objective.
- Reciting a framework instead of building a tree for this client.
- Overlapping branches, which leads to counting the same thing twice.
- Diving into a calculation without saying where it fits.
- Ending on a summary of the analysis instead of a recommendation.
- Forgetting risks and next steps.

## Key takeaways

- Clarify the objective, the business and the constraints before anything else.
- Build an issue tree whose branches are problem-focused, neutral and MECE.
- Use formulas for quantitative questions and grouping for qualitative ones.
- Know the classic case types, but adapt the structure to the client.
- Prioritise with a hypothesis and end every analysis with its implication.
- Conclude answer first: recommendation, reasons, risks, next steps.
